Predicting Wikipedia editor dropout at scale

Paper · CSECS 2026

Co-author with Aryan Meena, advised by Prof. F. Alizadeh-Sabdiz · accepted at EAI CSECS 2026, Boston, Nov 3–5 · Springer LNICST

The problem

Wikipedia runs on volunteers, and most newcomers stop editing within months. The Wikimedia Foundation funds research on this because article coverage depends on it. Earlier studies ran on a single machine, which capped them at samples small enough to fit in memory.

We asked whether an editor's first ten edits predict whether they will be gone six months later, across the full English Wikipedia history from 2021 to 2025: 25 GB compressed, 60 million revision events, 1.15 million editors. That is roughly ten times the scale of prior single-machine work.

What I owned

The feature representation and the model benchmark. I designed the 13-feature behavioural vector and the rule that made it defensible: every feature has to be computable from an editor's first ten edits alone. Anything drawn from their later history would leak the outcome we were predicting and quietly inflate our numbers. I then implemented and benchmarked the three models, including logistic regression and K-Means written directly on Spark RDDs rather than called from MLlib, so the comparison was ours end to end.

What made it hard

Scale changes which mistakes are cheap. A leaky feature on a laptop costs a rerun; here a full pass over 60 million events costs cluster time, so the feature set had to be right before the first run rather than after. The live scorer brought the opposite problem: a streaming event has no window around it, so features defined over an editor's history had to be replaced with single-event proxies that keep the same schema the batch models were trained on.

The result is the first reproducible, fully cloud-native benchmark for this problem at this scale. Same features, two paths: batch training on Dataproc, and a live scorer reading Wikipedia's edit stream through Kafka with end-to-end latency under a minute.

The most accurate model was the wrong one to ship

Solo

Cost-sensitive model selection for cardiovascular screening · 70,000 patients · BU MET CS 577

The problem

A primary-care doctor has about fifteen minutes to decide whether to refer a patient to a cardiologist. Standard practice in a modelling project is to train several classifiers and ship the most accurate one. That is the wrong objective here, because the two mistakes cost wildly different amounts: sending a sick patient home runs to roughly $150,000 in emergency care, while an unnecessary referral costs about $300.

A 500:1 asymmetry means accuracy, which weights both errors equally, cannot be the deciding metric.

What I owned

All of it, and the framing most of all. Re-ranking six trained classifiers by expected clinical cost rather than accuracy reverses the leaderboard: KNN, fifth of six on accuracy, becomes the safest choice. I then stress-tested that conclusion instead of stopping at it, sweeping the false-negative cost from $25K to $200K (the ranking holds throughout), auditing every model for gender fairness, and injecting 20% measurement noise to see which models survive.

What went wrong first

The raw data was worse than it looked. Eight sanity checks turned up blood pressures of 16,020 mmHg, negative diastolic values, heights of 250 cm, and ages stored in days. A clean-looking accuracy score sat on top of records that were physically impossible, so cleaning rules had to come before any model comparison was worth reading.

The practical finding for a fifteen-minute consult: blood pressure, cholesterol and age alone recover 99.5% of full-model accuracy, so the screening version needs three measurements rather than thirteen.

Why SMOTE failed on 778 one-hot features

Team of two

Disability prediction on the 2023 American Housing Survey · with Jai Sharma · BU MET CS 699

The problem

Predict whether a U.S. household includes someone with a disability, from 144 Census survey variables where only 23% of households are positive. With that imbalance, a model that always answers "no" scores 76.6% accuracy and is useless.

What I owned

Two pieces the rest of the project depended on. First, variable typing from the codebook: reading all 313 pages showed that 72% of the columns Python reports as numeric are actually category codes, where NOISE = 1 means yes and 2 means no. Standardising those as numbers would have corrupted every model downstream while still producing plausible-looking scores. That audit set the encoding strategy: 55 binary, 56 categorical, 32 genuinely numeric. Second, the oversampling track (SMOTE, ADASYN, SMOTE-75%, NearMiss) plus deep tuning of the gradient boosting and random forest models in phase three.

What went wrong, and why it mattered

My oversampling models failed, consistently: 95%+ on the majority class but only 30–46% recall on the households we cared about. The useful part was working out why rather than dropping them. One-hot encoding pushes the data to 778 mostly binary dimensions; SMOTE interpolates between neighbours and produces fractional values for yes/no fields, inventing households that cannot exist. Euclidean distance across one-hot categories is close to meaningless, so its "nearest neighbours" aren't similar households at all. The synthetic data looks more separable than reality, and the models learn a boundary that doesn't survive contact with the test set.

Undersampling won by discarding half the majority class and keeping only real households, which is counter-intuitive until you see the numbers. That negative result is now the most useful paragraph in our report.

How I work

Pick the metric that matches the cost

Accuracy is usually the easiest metric and rarely the right one. The question I ask first is what each kind of mistake costs the person on the other end.

→ KNN over Random Forest: $9.7M per 10K patients

Distrust a result that flatters you

A surprising number is a reason to check the setup, not to celebrate. Twice now the check overturned the headline, and both times the corrected version was the more interesting finding.

→ the 120× database gap that was my own bug

Do the unglamorous data work first

Codebooks, impossible values, leakage. These decide whether a model means anything, and they are invisible in the final score, which is exactly why they get skipped.
