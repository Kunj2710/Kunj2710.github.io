Predicting Wikipedia editor dropout at scale

Paper · CSECS 2026

with Aryan Meena, advised by Prof. F. Alizadeh-Sabdiz · Boston, Nov 3–5 · Springer LNICST

Wikipedia runs on volunteers and keeps losing its newcomers. Prior studies fit on one machine, which capped them at small samples. We asked whether an editor's first ten edits predict whether they are gone six months later, across the full English history from 2021 to 2025: 25 GB compressed, 60 million revision events, 1.15 million editors. Roughly ten times the scale of previous single-machine work.

My part

The feature representation and the model benchmark. I designed the 13-feature behavioural vector and the constraint that makes it defensible: every feature computable from the first ten edits alone. Anything from an editor's later history leaks the outcome and quietly inflates the score. Then I implemented the three models, with logistic regression and K-Means written on Spark RDDs rather than called from MLlib.

What made it hard

Scale changes which mistakes are cheap. A leaky feature on a laptop costs a rerun; here a full pass over 60 million events costs cluster time, so the feature set had to be right before the first run. Streaming brought the opposite problem: a live event has no window around it, so history-based features needed single-event proxies that keep the schema the batch models trained on.

The most accurate model was the wrong one to ship

Solo

cost-sensitive model selection · 70,000 patients · BU MET CS 577

A doctor has about fifteen minutes to decide whether to refer a patient to a cardiologist. The usual move is to train several classifiers and ship the most accurate. That is the wrong objective here: sending a sick patient home costs roughly $150,000 in emergency care, an unnecessary referral about $300. Accuracy weights both errors equally, so it cannot be the deciding metric.

My part

All of it, and the framing most of all. Re-ranking the six trained models by expected cost reverses the leaderboard. Then I stress-tested that instead of stopping at it: sweeping the false-negative cost from $25K to $200K, auditing every model for gender fairness, and injecting 20% measurement noise to see which survive. Drag the slider and watch the ranking move.

What went wrong first

The raw data was worse than it looked. Eight checks turned up blood pressures of 16,020 mmHg, negative diastolic values, heights of 250 cm and ages stored in days. Clean-looking accuracy sat on top of physically impossible records, so the cleaning rules had to come before any comparison was worth reading.

Why SMOTE failed on 778 one-hot features

Team of two

disability prediction, 2023 American Housing Survey · with Jai Sharma · BU MET CS 699

Predict whether a U.S. household includes someone with a disability, from 144 Census variables where only 23% are positive. At that imbalance a model that always answers "no" scores 76.6% and is useless. We ran 70+ configurations: five balancing methods, nine classifiers, three tuning phases.

My part

Two pieces the rest depended on. Reading all 313 pages of the codebook showed that 72% of the columns Python calls numeric are category codes, where NOISE = 1 means yes and 2 means no. Standardising those as numbers corrupts every model downstream while still producing plausible scores. That audit set the encoding: 55 binary, 56 categorical, 32 genuinely numeric. I also owned the oversampling track and the deep tuning of gradient boosting and random forest.

What went wrong, and why it mattered

My oversampled models failed consistently: 95%+ on the majority class, 30–46% recall on the households we cared about. The useful part was working out why. One-hot encoding pushes the data into 778 mostly binary dimensions, and SMOTE interpolates between neighbours, producing fractions for yes/no fields: households that cannot exist. Undersampling won by keeping only real rows. That negative result is now the most useful paragraph in our report.
